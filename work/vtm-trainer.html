Redesigning the operator trainer

The trainer teaches car-factory operators to pick the right tool and part for every step on the line. Its interface had grown piece by piece. I redesigned it and tested the prototype before release.

Role
UI design, prototype, test plan and analysis
When
Autumn 2019, Vizendo
Users
Assembly operators; training engineers who author content
Methods
Interactive prototype, component-by-component survey, thematic coding

The problem

Operators use the trainer to rehearse an assembly sequence before they do it on the line. For each operation they choose a tool and a part; if they get it wrong, the software shows them the right one with a blue flashing highlight. Around that loop sit an operation list, the operation text, information about the sequence and a help menu.

Each of those had been added when a customer needed it, and it showed. Panels used different backgrounds and sizes, the layout had been designed for one desktop resolution, and it now had to work on tablets as well.

The goal was a consistent, modern interface that worked from an iPad up to a 4K screen.

What I did

I rebuilt the layout around the core loop: tool and part selection stays dominant, everything else moves to the edges and collapses when it isn't needed. Help moved behind a single button. Panels got one shared visual treatment.

Testing it

Before handing it to development I put the clickable prototype in front of twelve colleagues from across the company, people who build, sell and support the trainer. For each part of the interface I asked two open questions: how does it work, and how does it look. Keeping them apart made it easier to separate usability problems from taste.

I then coded every free-text answer by theme. Some answers touched several themes.

  • Icons weren't self-explanatory; testers asked for labels on hover7
  • Sizes didn't scale well across tablet, 1080p and 4K6
  • Hidden interactions, like double-click to enlarge a part, weren't discoverable6
  • Panels still didn't look consistent5
  • Hard to tell the part you selected from the correct one when both were highlighted4

I wouldn't understand any of those icons for a first time user.

Tester, about the help menu
The finding that changed my thinking

One tester pointed out that a question-mark icon usually means "read the manual". In the trainer, help means "I'm stuck on the line right now". The icon was correct by convention and wrong for the situation.

The feedback also confirmed what worked. Testers called the new look clearer and calmer, and the sequence panel's "last updated" date turned out to matter to people supporting customers: it let them check whether a customer had pushed new files before blaming the software.

What changed

Fill in: what you changed after the test. For example hover labels on icons, minimum sizes for tablets, a visible zoom affordance, a clearer selected vs. correct state, a stronger help button. Name what shipped and what was left out.

What I'd do differently

I tested with colleagues because they were available, and they knew the old trainer far better than a new operator would. Asking what people thought of each panel also produced more opinions than evidence.

Today I would give a handful of real operators a short, timed task, such as completing one sequence with a mistake built in, and watch where they hesitate. The survey was still worth doing: it was cheap, it came back in two days, and the strongest themes were exactly the kind a task-based test would have shown.
