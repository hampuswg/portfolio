A prosthetic arm for Nairobi

3D Life Prints wanted to improve their 3D-printed arm. Interviews in Sweden and eight weeks of fieldwork in Kenya showed that the real problem was somewhere else.

Role
Industrial designer, with Mikael Carlström
When
2016, 20 weeks; then employed to continue
Client
3D Life Prints, Nairobi
Methods
Interviews, questionnaire, contextual observation, AEIOU, requirements, prototyping

The brief

In Kenya, conventional prostheses are expensive and the specialists who fit them are few. 3D Life Prints, a startup working in East Africa, was printing body-powered arms on desktop 3D printers in Nairobi and asked us to improve the design: better parts, more value.

This was our master thesis in Industrial Design Engineering at Luleå University of Technology, funded in part by a Sida Minor Field Study scholarship.

Research in two countries

Neither of us knew prosthetics, so we started with literature, a questionnaire to prosthetists and orthotists in Sweden, and semi-structured interviews with Swedish prosthesis users and experts.

In Nairobi we interviewed stakeholders, prosthetists and users, worked inside the 3D Life Prints workshop and made two field visits to Kenya Medical Training College to observe students making prostheses. We structured our notes with the AEIOU framework: activities, environments, interactions, objects and users.

The most important part for me is the relationship with my technician, to know that if something breaks I can get it fixed.

Prosthesis user, Sweden

What we learned

  • Durability beats features. Every user we interviewed raised durability. Each extra mechanism is one more thing that can leave someone without their arm.
  • Blending in matters to everyone at some point, even people who said it didn't. In Kenya the literature suggested it mattered even more.
  • Independence in daily tasks was what users valued most. We called this group "daily users", and added farming, the largest occupation, as a key activity.
  • Price decides who gets one at all. Material cost and printing time drive what a clinic can offer.
Reframing the problem

We arrived to add value to the arm. On site it became clear that no patient had yet been fitted with it. The real problem wasn't missing features; it was getting a reliable product that could be printed, assembled and repaired locally.

Designing for manufacture

We turned our findings into a list of requirements, ran brainstorming and brain-drawing sessions, developed three concepts and prototyped them on the workshop's printers. The final design reworked the three main components: the hand's internal insert, the harness and the socket.

−9.4 hprinting time for the socket, from 22.4 to 13.1 hours
−132 SEKmaterial cost per arm, mainly from a simpler harness
1 plateall rigid parts now print in a single run

The biggest saving came from replacing expensive bicycle brake cable in the harness with fishing line. A flexible material that often caused failed prints was replaced by semi-flexible nylon.

What happened next

After graduating I was hired by 3D Life Prints as a design engineer and kept developing the LifeArm until spring 2017.

Looking back

Reaching the primary users in Kenya was much harder than we expected, so most of our direct user input came from Sweden and from experts. I'd plan recruitment much earlier now. I'd also check our design against the business more critically: the new process needed an extra printer at every clinic, which wasn't obviously worth it.

What stuck with me is that the most useful thing a designer can do is sometimes to question the brief.
