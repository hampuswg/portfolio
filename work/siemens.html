Owning a training product at Siemens

Since 2021 I've been product manager for a suite of software that large manufacturers use to train assembly operators. Much of the job is deciding what users actually need from a long list of what customers ask for.

Role
Product manager
When
2021 to now
Users
Assembly operators and the engineers who author their training
Work
Strategy, roadmap, prioritisation, requirements, compliance

The product

The suite includes tools for authoring training content and the trainer that operators use on desktops and tablets, deployed across customers' plants. Customers are global automotive and manufacturing companies, and the people using it range from engineers building content to operators in their first week on the line.

I came with the product from Vizendo, so I've seen it from the content side, the customer side and now the product side.

From requests to a roadmap

Change requests arrive constantly, from several large customers with different processes. Taken one by one, each is reasonable. Built all together, they'd turn the product into a pile of special cases, which is exactly the problem I had to clean up in the trainer redesign.

So I try to get under each request to the job the user is trying to do, group requests that share a root cause, and weigh them against where the product needs to be in two or three years.

Designing within real constraints

Enterprise software for factories comes with requirements end users never see: a formal product lifecycle process, quality management, cybersecurity compliance and data privacy. Part of my job is making sure those are built in from the start rather than bolted on, without making the product harder to use.

Why this matters for UX

Product management taught me to frame problems, prioritise and work with developers, sales and customers at once. What I want now is to spend more of my time on the part I enjoy most: understanding users and designing the interface itself.
